The ground under Regensburg

Why a medieval city on the Danube sits on gravel, why the hills across the river are granite, and where the green and white stones of its cathedral came from.

Real

Everything on this page is real. The small blue numbers link to the source list, which says exactly what each source was used for.

Where four kinds of ground meet

Regensburg stands at the Danube's northernmost point, where the Naab and the Regen join it.10 The city also holds a key place in Bavarian geology: the old crystalline basement of eastern Bavaria, the layered scarplands to the west and the Molasse basin south of the Danube all meet here.1

In one walk you can cross from granite hills to limestone cliffs to the gravel plain of the river. The drawing below shows how they fit together.

Seas that came and went

The Jurassic sea first reached the Regensburg bay in the Early Jurassic. A last wide flooding in the Late Jurassic (Malm), about 160 million years ago, spread far over the crystalline basement east of the city.1

A long spell as land followed. Then the Cenomanian sea pushed north from the south. It withdrew in the Santonian, and the Regensburg area has been land ever since.1 At the Schutzfelsen near Pentling a single river slope shows the result: karstified Upper Jurassic massive limestones at the bottom, Regensburger Grünsandstein above them, then Upper Cretaceous sandstones, marls and limestones.2

Faults: why the hills stop at the river

North-east of the city the gneiss and granite hills are framed by faults: the Pfahl in the north, the Keilberg fault in the west and the Danube boundary fault, the Donaurandbruch, in the south.1 The Donaurandbruch is a strong fault zone that separates the Molasse basin from the crystalline basement.4 Since the end of the Cretaceous the Bavarian Forest has been pushed up again along this edge.1

The faults still shape the land. The steep slope of the Winzerer Höhen was laid out by the Donaurandbruch, and landslides there have been described for decades.3

Stone for a medieval city

Limestone

Light, massive limestone

The cathedral's oldest surviving parts, remains of the older cathedral's early 11th-century transept, are built only of light, massive limestone. Builders chose it because it was easy to bring in and to work.5

Grünsandstein

Green sandstone

From around 1480 Grünsandstein was also used in load-bearing parts of the towers and west front, probably because the old limestone quarries were running out.5 While the main portal was built, the stone changed from fine white limestone to coarse Abbach Grünsandstein.6

Grünsandstein has a bad name for weathering, partly because the wrong stone was picked in the quarry.7 Air pollution adds to the damage: sulphur compounds from smoke form crusts that break the cathedral's stones down.5

The Stone Bridge was built in about eleven years, probably 1135–1146. It has been under restoration since 2010, and a suitable sandstone for the repairs was eventually found in an abandoned quarry near Ihrlerstein.8

The Danube and its floods

In the last glaciation the Danube built up to three low gravel terraces in its valley below Regensburg: NT1 at the height of the glaciation, NT2 in its early to late phase and NT3 at its end. Seven Holocene floodplain levels lie below them.4 The terraces stand clear of normal floods. The floodplain does not.

In July 1342, around the feast of St Mary Magdalene on 22 July, a flood struck the Rhine, Main, Danube, Mosel and other rivers. Regensburg is one of the towns whose chronicles record it.9 Researchers estimate that about 13 billion tonnes of soil were washed off the land, as much as 2,000 years of normal weather would remove.9

What this means under Neupfarrplatz

Neupfarrplatz

No soil or geological study of the ground under Neupfarrplatz is published online. What the sources do say is that the remains of the Roman camp lie about 6 m below the square, so the top six metres there are ground made by people.18 See NP-01 and the layers.
