Regensburg · Middle Ages · ordinary people

How a peasant was buried

From the deathbed to the churchyard and, a few generations later, the charnel house: what Bavarian sources tell us about burying an ordinary person, and what real graves dug up in and around Regensburg show.

Real · sourced

The steps below come from historians' accounts of burial customs in medieval Bavaria and German-speaking Europe. The graves further down are real ones excavated in and around Regensburg. No written account of one particular Regensburg peasant's funeral is published online, so this page puts the general customs and the local finds side by side rather than inventing one.

Two ways of burial

c. 450 – 700

Row-grave cemeteries

In the early Middle Ages the dead were buried in cemeteries away from the houses, often with goods in the grave. Around Burgweinting, now part of Regensburg, archaeologists found six such cemeteries with well over 300 burials from between 450 and 700.25 Rich grave furnishings were given up early in the Middle Ages.24

From the late 7th century

The churchyard

Burial moved to churchyards inside the settlement, around the church, so that the dead lay near the saints and the living. In southern Bavaria the shift began as early as the late 7th century; in north-eastern Bavaria cemeteries outside the settlements lasted much longer.24 In Regensburg itself, people were buried at St. Emmeram and St. Rupert from the late 6th century until modern times.26

The steps, from deathbed to charnel house

For a churchyard burial in the High and late Middle Ages. A peasant belonged to what the sources call the wider population: buried in the churchyard, not inside the church.24

  1. Before death

    Caring for the dying

    Care for the dying was already central to preparing for what came after death. Relatives and, where there were any, household servants usually looked after the dying person.24 Books on the "art of dying well" (ars moriendi) appeared only in the late Middle Ages.24

  2. Right after death

    Washing and preparing the body

    Sources mention washing the dead again and again, though mostly for the elite. For ordinary people, it was probably relatives and servants who prepared the body.24

  3. Before the funeral

    Wrapping in a shroud

    The Church held up a simple burial as the model for lay people: a saint's life tells of a woman who asked for nothing more than a shroud and a veil.24 Excavated churchyard graves in German-speaking Europe include burials in shrouds and in coffins.27 In large studies elsewhere in Europe, coffins became more common and grave goods rarer as the Middle Ages went on.28

  4. Funeral day

    A Mass for the dead

    After the body was washed and prepared, a Mass for the dead was said before the burial. In Bavaria this is recorded as early as 776, in Freising.24

  5. Funeral day

    Into the churchyard

    Medieval Christian burial meant a single grave on shared ground, the body laid out on its back, oriented west to east with the face looking east.27 By the High Middle Ages the eastward orientation was part of the Church's burial rules.24 The churchyard was consecrated and enclosed, and the Church could refuse burial there to some people.24

  6. Afterwards

    A wooden cross, not a stone

    The clerical and secular elite were laid in lasting tombs inside the church, with grave slabs. Most people got short-lived markers such as wooden crosses, and from the 11th century this difference marked social rank ever more sharply.24

  7. Years afterwards

    Prayers for the soul

    The dead were remembered in prayer while their souls were thought to be still being purified. When the abbot Odilo of Cluny introduced All Souls' Day at the beginning of the 11th century, this remembrance spread far more widely among lay people.24

  8. About two generations later

    Making room: the charnel house

    A churchyard inside a settlement soon filled up, so the dead had to be dug up regularly. From the High Middle Ages this led to charnel houses, and exhumation after roughly two generations became common for most of the dead.24 A charnel house (Karner or Beinhaus) is a building in a cemetery that held bones dug up to make room for new graves, usually with a chapel above it.29 In Bavaria the charnel house became a regional building tradition in the late Middle Ages.24

Real graves in and around Regensburg

Burgweinting · late 7th century

A farmstead and its 28 graves

In the late 1980s archaeologists dug a farmstead about 700 m east of Burgweinting's village centre: three larger buildings and a sunken hut with sherds of the late 7th century. Its 28 graves held only a few datable goods and were far more poorly furnished than the nearby cemeteries of the rich.25 This is the closest the record comes to a peasant family's own burial ground.

Burgweinting · c. 450 – 700

Six cemeteries, 300 burials

Five of the six cemeteries lay in the Kirchfeld. In the oldest, some of the dead lay in tree-trunk coffins: split, hollowed logs. In the largest, with more than 200 burials from about 500 to 700, almost every grave had been robbed, which was common practice at the time.25

Burgweinting · High Middle Ages

A churchyard on the hill

In the medieval village, the parish and the cemetery laid out around it stayed at St. Michael, the church that still stands on a prominent rise.25 Both of the village's medieval churches stand near the early medieval cemeteries.25

Regensburg · late 6th century onwards

St. Emmeram and St. Rupert

Inside the city, people were buried at St. Emmeram and St. Rupert in a very tight space, without a break, from the late 6th century into modern times.26

Regensburg · to the 7th century

The Great Cemetery

The huge cemetery along today's railway lines, begun in Roman times, was used into the 7th century, and its graves lie several on top of one another. In the 19th century about 5,000 graves were recorded on an area of 120 × 100 m, most of them cremations.30

Around Regensburg · early Middle Ages

Many kinds of burial ground

A city archive study of six early medieval sites sets a typical Bavarian row-grave cemetery at Geisling beside a noble cemetery with burial mounds at Harting, a family burial place in the ruins of a Roman bath, a farmstead burial ground at Burgweinting and a cemetery at Weichs used only briefly around 700.26

Not everyone was buried this way

Regensburg's Jewish community followed its own law: it buried its dead quickly, in a cemetery outside the settlement walls.24 That cemetery was desecrated in 1519 and its gravestones were taken for building (see NP-08 and NP-09).
